\begin{abstract}
The hidden service directories (HSDirs) of Tor's third-generation (V3) onion service protocol store and serve the descriptors that make onion 
services reachable, yet their reliability and operator structure in deployment are more often specified than measured. This work presents an empirical, 
one-month measurement of modern V3 HSDirs, observing the network-wide directory consensus together with a selected and controlled population of static, ephemeral and 
third-party onion services. The method combines passive hourly auditing of the directory consensus with active descriptor probing, the latter exposing silent 
failures in which a directory listed as healthy cannot serve the descriptor it should hold. Across uptime, churn, operator identity, hash-ring position and 
descriptor hosting, the directory population is found to be highly stable in aggregate despite substantial daily churn, and uniformly distributed across 
the hash ring even where sybil concentration is strong. A single declared family dominates every measured population without translating that concentration 
into disproportionate ring-position control, and its failures appear as ongoing partial churn rather than a collapse of the entire operator. On this basis the study 
shows that a single-node failure can be distinguished from an operator-level failure through family and network-prefix clustering, and that combining 
consensus auditing with active probing uncovers operator structure and silent hosting failures that a consensus-only view would miss.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Tor is among the most widely used overlay networks for anonymous communication. Beyond protecting the users who initiate connections, it also provides a 
mechanism for protecting the operators of the services being reached. Onion services, first introduced in the mid-2000s and deployed in what became the 
second-generation (V2) design, allow a service to be reached without revealing its network location~\cite{dingledine2004tor}. Each onion service is 
identified by a self-generated address that clients use to locate it, with the connection carried over a multi-hop circuit so that neither end learns the 
other's identity or position.
 
The V2 design was formally deprecated by the Tor Project, with the withdrawal completed in 2021~\cite{tor_v2_deprecation}, in favour of a third-generation (V3) 
design that addresses vulnerabilities identified in the earlier protocol. In summary, V2 relied on cryptographic primitives now considered weak and exposed onion 
addresses to the directory relays that indexed them, which enabled address harvesting and enumeration by anyone operating or targeting those 
relays~\cite{biryukov2013trawling}. V3 replaces the cryptography with modern primitives, restructures publication so that a directory no longer learns the 
addresses it stores, and reduces the corresponding attack surface. The mechanics of the V3 scheme are specified by the Tor Project~\cite{tor_rendspec, tor_dirspec} 
and are summarised in the background section.

The directories at the centre of this design, the hidden service directories (HSDirs), are ordinary Tor relays that have earned the responsibility of storing and 
serving onion service descriptors. Their reliability and their operator structure therefore bear directly on the availability and security of every V3 service, 
yet they are more often described from the protocol specification than measured in deployment. This work examines the current state of V3 HSDirs through direct measurement. Over 
a continuous one-month window it observes both the network-wide directory consensus and a selected and controlled set of onion services of three kinds, persistent (static) 
services, short-lived (ephemeral) services, and third-party services operated by others, recording which directories host their descriptors and how the directory 
population behaves over time. Alongside passive consensus auditing, the collection infrastructure actively probes for descriptors, which exposes silent failures 
in which a directory listed as healthy cannot actually serve the descriptor it should hold. The mechanics of this probing are described in the methodology and in 
the engineered collection tool.
 
The Evaluation is organised around six analytical themes, the basis and scope of the measurement. Directory reliability in terms of uptime and stability, churn 
and flag dynamics, operator identity and sybil concentration, position within the hash ring, and descriptor-hosting behaviour, including upload-versus-fetch 
persistence and silent hosting failures. Together these address a single central question, determining whether an observed directory failure can be attributed 
to the loss of a single node or to the loss of an entire operator, where an operator is identified by its Sybil footprint across family identifiers and network 
prefixes. Specific measurements and their interpretation are developed in the evaluation and drawn together in the conclusions.

\section{Background}

This section presents the Tor's onion service V3 protocol with an overview of the essential parties of the
mechanism, including the onion services address generation, publication and indexing, and connection establishment \cite{tor_rendspec, tor_onion_overview}.

\subsection{Mechanism and Protocol Overview}

The architectural ecosystem of the Tor version 3 (V3) onion service protocol implements a decentralized framework designed to facilitate mutual anonymized application-layer
communication for both the client and the service provider while masking the identities and locations of both endpoints. Different from centralized domain registries, the V3
architecture operates entirely within the Tor relay network, managing connections through a specialized set of relays avoiding direct network visilibility of both endpoints.
The public service address is splitted from their current IP routing path with a transient overlay and a multi-hop circuit \cite{dingledine2004tor}. The framework relies essential in the interaction
of six operational entities distributed across the network \cite{tor_rendspec, tor_onion_overview}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Tor Client:} The end-user application seeking to establish an authenticated, secure connection to the onion service without disclosing its own identity. Commonly a local proxy dawmon that encapsulates the traffic, handles keys exchanges and establishes multi-hop virtual paths to mask the origins requests.
    \item \textbf{Onion Service:} The destination instance and isolated server hosting a service or application endpoints that listen uniquely to incoming traffic through a pre-arranged anonymous path while preserving the confidentility of its public IP address and network topology.
    \item \textbf{Directory Authorities:} A cluster of core trusted infrastructure server nodes that collaboratively manage and monitor the network consensus status, calculate the daily Shared Random Values (SRVs), and issue the the network consensus directory each hour required for layout lookups \cite{tor_dirspec, tor_srvspec}.
    \item \textbf{Introduction Point - IPO:} A set of publicly accessible Tor relays arbitrarily selected by the onion service to maintain long-lived and persistent circuits. The operational duty of these nodes is to accept received incoming initialization requests from clients and proxying them to the service via pre-established circuits without knowing its actual location.
    \item \textbf{Hidden Service Directory - HSDir:} A dynamic subset of Tor relays that meet strict stability and uptime criteria. These nodes are authorized to store and serve signed cryptographic service descriptors within a decentralized and time dependent Distributed Hash Table (DHT) structure known as Hash Ring \cite{tor_rendspec, tor_dirspec}.
    \item \textbf{Rendezvous Point - RPO:} Ephemeral relays dynamically chosen by the client on the spot. These nodes act as the neutral nexus where the circuit branches from the client and service safely converge.
\end{enumerate}

The chronological lifecycle required to establish an end-to-end connection between the client and a V3 onion service transitions through a six cryptographic and operational stages \cite{tor_rendspec, tor_onion_overview}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{IPO Setup:} Upon initialization, the V3 onion service generates its root identity key pairs, derives its cryptographic \texttt{.onion} address and establishes individual three-hop circuits to multiple chosen public relays to act as active IPOs.
    \item \textbf{Descriptor Publication:} The service generates an onion service descriptor containing its public verification keys and the network routing parameters of its IPOs. This document is cryptographically signed using an Ed25519 key-blinding mechanism that daily rotates, and uploaded to a default of eight designated HSDir positions derived mathematically on the Hash Ring.
    \item \textbf{Discovery and Descriptor Retrieval:} The client to reach the target onion address queries the active network consensus along the global SRV to pinpoint and compute the exact HSDir relays responsible of hosting the service's current descriptor and downloading the authenticated record over an anonymous circuit.
    \item \textbf{Rendezvous Establishment:} The client designates an independent Tor relay from the active consensus pool to act as RPO, builds a separate three-hoop circuit to it and establish a single-use cryptographic secret denominated as rendezvous cookie.
    \item \textbf{Introduction Delivery:} The client dispatches an encrypted cell (\texttt{INTRODUCE1}), containing the RPO routing descriptors and the rendezvous cookie and encrypted using the service's public key, to one of the service's published IPOs, which blindly forwards it through its pre-established circuit to the hidden service.
    \item \textbf{Circuit Splicing:} Upon decrypting the cell, the onion service parses the IPO cell, establishes a newly three-hop circuit to the specified RPO and handovers a validation via a (\texttt{RENDEZVOUS1}) cell. The RPO then confirms the cookie matches and joints the two independent streams into a unified six-hop end-to-end circuit.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Address Generation}

The Tor version 3 (V3) onion routing introduces a more cryptographically and decentralized naming convention replacing the legacy 16-character version 2 (V2) address space which derivation introduced significant cryptographic
vulnerabilities including partial-match dictionary collisions, namespace harvesting and lack of support for forward-secure operations \cite{tor_v2_deprecation, biryukov2013trawling}. The V3 protocol remediates these vulnerabilities by transitioning to Ed25519 elliptic curve
cryptography mapping the public key directly into the string encoding of the public identifier serving as self-authentication. This shift increases the \texttt{.onion} address length into a 56-character base32-encoded string,
eliminating central name authorities dependencies and preventing unauthorized directory harversting from mapping or enumerating hidden application endpoints while proving ownership \cite{tor_rendspec}.

The generation of a valid V3 onion address consists on the congregation of a 35-byte binary payload composed of three crucial fields, then serialized using Base32 encoding \cite{tor_rendspec}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Master Public Key (32 bytes):} The Ed25519 master public key ($\textit{P}_{k}$) generated by the onion service during its initialization phase and root identity of the hidden service.
    \item \textbf{Verification Checksum (2 bytes):} A truncated security chunk ($\textit{C}_{s}$) calculated derived from cryptographic hash function to ensure string integrity and protect against typos or transmission distortions.
    \item \textbf{Protocol Version (1 byte):} A fixed administrative byte ($\textit{V}$) indicating the technical generation of the protocol explicitly configured to \texttt{0x03} for Tor version 3 (V3).
\end{enumerate}

To calculate the 2-byte verification checksum, the protocol combines a predefined static string \texttt{".onion checksum"} as a fixed salt, the 32-byte master public key, and the the protocol version byte into a unified byte stream. 
The stream is processed using the SHA3-256 hashing algorithm, which resulting digest is truncated to its first two bytes \cite{tor_rendspec}:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}_{\text{s}} = \text{SHA3-256}\big(\textit{".onion checksum"} \parallel \textit{P}_{\text{k}} \parallel \textit{V}\big)\big[:2\big]
\end{equation}

Once the checksum ($\textit{C}_{\text{s}}$) is derived, it is appended to the master public key and the protocol version byte to construct the 35-byte vector. The resulting domain name is produced by converting the 
byte vector into an Base32 sequence with the domain suffix appended:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{A}_{\text{onion}} = \text{Base32}\big(\textit{P}_{\text{k}} \parallel \textit{C}_{\text{s}} \parallel \textit{V}\big) \parallel \textit{".onion"}
\end{equation}

This infrastructure produces direct implications for network telemetry and monitoring. Because the resulting onion address wraps the raw master key ($\textit{P}_{k}$), any external system that captures the service's address
can extract the inherent cryptographic key. The master identity key acts as the fundamental parameter to calculate daily blinded keys, which govern the exact time dependent coordinates the service will occupy on the network's HRT
over the given lifecycle.

\subsection{Publication and Indexing}\label{subsec:pub-n-index}

To shield hidden services from targeted denial-of-service (DoS) vectors and metadata harversting by adversary directory servers \cite{biryukov2013trawling}, the Tor version 3 (V3) introduces a key-blinding subsystem along a consistent distributed hashing topology \cite{tor_rendspec}.
Rather than uploading static routing records to fixed servers, V3 onion services routinely rotates ephemeral blinded public keys ($\textit{K}_{b}$) to dynamically compute a set of time dependent storage nodes within the HRT. Services
ensure confidentiality by serializing active routing vectors and cryptographic information in a structural document known as onion descriptor, protected with an architectural encryption to prevent intermediate nodes from harvesting onion addresses
or identify the services being hosted by it unless they already posses the corresponding credentials.

The derivation of blinded credentials, spatial positioning and temporal rotation descriptors accross the global HRT shifts are governed by two synchronized parameters daily updated within the network consensus, the global SRV and the time period, or temporal epoch \cite{tor_rendspec}.
The consensus cluster of directory authorities produce a newly generated SRV every consecutive interval (epoch) defined by the consensus parameter \texttt{hsdir-interval}, which defaults to an operational duration of 1440 minutes (24 hours), at 00:00 UTC over hourly voting intervals \cite{tor_srvspec}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Commit Phase (00:00 - 12:00 UTC):} Authorities include cryptographic commitments of privetly generated random numbers within their hourly consensus votes.
    \item \textbf{Reveal Phase (12:00 - 00:00 UTC):} Authorities expose their random values, validating them against the previous phase's commitments. At 00:00 UTC, the final unified SRV is compiled and appended to the live consensus.
\end{enumerate}

This shared secret acts a global seed that inforces pseudo-random reshuffling of the entire hash ring, preventing pre-calculation and maintance of malicious nodes adjacent to a target's publication space \cite{tor_srvspec}.

To publish its onion descriptor, the hidden service applies a key-blinding function mixed with the two current synchronized temporal variables to produce the temporal blinded public key $\textit{K}_{\text{b}}$ \cite{tor_rendspec}. For each independent publication replica the service derives a distinct 256-bit
coordinate refer as the service index, where the number of replicas ($\textit{N}_{\text{r}}$) defaults to 2 replicas. The derivation of the service index for a given replica identifier ($\textit{r}$) is as follows:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{HS}_{\text{index}} = \text{SHA3-256}\big(\textit{"store-at-idx"} \parallel \textit{K}_{\text{b}} \parallel \textit{r} \parallel \textit{L}_{\text{p}} \parallel \textit{N}_{\text{p}}\big)
\end{equation}

where $\textit{K}_{\text{blinded}}$ represents the blinded public key, $\textit{L}_{\text{p}}$ is the standardized length of the temporal epoch, and $\textit{N}_{\text{p}}$ is the sequential index of the current time period. 

Concurrently, every active relay in the consensus that satisfies specific uptime, stability, and flag requirements (\texttt{HSDir, Stable, Fast}) to operate as HSDir computes its own relative position on the ring, recalculated for each temporal epoch using the global SRV \cite{tor_rendspec, tor_dirspec}:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}_{\text{index}} = \text{SHA3-256}\big(\textit{"node-idx"} \parallel \textit{ID} \parallel \text{SRV} \parallel \textit{N}_{\text{p}} \parallel \textit{L}_{\text{p}}\big)
\end{equation}

where $\textit{ID}$ denotes the cryptographic fingerprint of the relay.

Once both indeces are mapped onto the 256-bit circular index table of the HRT, the onion identifies the specific relays in charge of reduntdantly storing the current onion descriptor. The service selects the first distinct HSDir nodes ($\textit{k}$) whose positions on the HRT 
($\mathcal{R}_{\text{index}}$) immediately succeed the hidden service positions ($\mathcal{HS}_{\text{index}}$), where the pool number of nodes ($\textit{k}$) is given by the consensus parameter \texttt{hsdir-spread-store} explicitly set to 4 \cite{tor_rendspec}. Thus, the service initiates a sequence
of encrypted descriptor uploads to these first 4 unique HSDir nodes indentified for each service index computed, resulting in the descriptor upload to total of 8 distinct HSDir nodes to host and serve the record during the designated time period.

Because these positions shift daily along the global SRV, this parameter along $\textit{N}_{\text{p}}$, $\textit{L}_{\text{p}}$ are publicly observable through the public network consensus files which serve to track a targeted \texttt{.onion} address with an independent system
to extract the onion's master key, compute $\textit{K}_{\text{b}}$, and determine the exact 8 HSDir relays responsible of hosting the onion's descriptor any given day. This mapping provides the basis to track HSDir node uptime distributions, assess runtime stability or operator entity
cluster profiling via IP range correlation \cite{winter2016sybil}.

\subsection{Service Access}

Unlike the classical Internet, where a client establishes a direct transport layer socket to an IP address, the Tor version 3 (V3) requires both the client and server to construct separate three-hop virtual circuits which congregation to a mutually agreed intermediate node is referred as
rendezvous mechanism \cite{tor_rendspec, tor_onion_overview}. This architecture ensures neither end can deduce the routing trajectory, geo-origin and underlying network topology of the opposite party. 

The service access process begins with the client parsing the 56-character \texttt{.onion} address to locate the master public identity key and resolves the inherent Ed25519 public key, which combined with the temporal epoch and SRV retrieved from the network status consensus, the client
cryptographically computes the target service $\textit{K}_{\text{b}}$ and derive the settled service indices mapped onto the HRT. The client then selects one of the four active HSDir relays appointed to the replica, constructs an anonymous three-hop circuit toward it, and transmits an authenticated
HTTP GET request to download the signed onion descriptor records \cite{tor_rendspec}.

Upon receiving the payload, the client parses the descriptor utilizing the blinded key structures to verify the descriptor's cryptographic signature and decrypt its inner layer. This extracts the array of active IPOs routing vectors along with their respective public keys and link specifiers, and the 
service's temporal encryption keys. The connection sequence then diverges into two parallel paths \cite{tor_rendspec}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Rendezvous Establishment:} The client selects an external relay from the consensus to act as an isolated cryptographic bridge referred as RPO. A three-hop circuit is constructed toward this node and delivered with an \texttt{ESTABLISH\_RENDEZVOUS} cell, which provisions the relay with a secret and an unique single-use 20-byte rendezvous cookie. Upon acknowledging the setup, the RPO registers the cookie in its active volatile session cache and awaits an inbound circuit connection that presents the corresponding token.
    \item \textbf{Introduction Delivery:} Simultaneously, the client constructs a three-hop circuit to one of the public IPOs specified in the decrypted descriptor records. An \texttt{INTRODUCE1} authenticated cell is then transmitted to it, which envelops an encrypted message containing the routing specifiers and the matching 20-byte rendezvous cookie encrypted and safeguarded under the service's onion key against intermediate inspection.
\end{enumerate}

Upon receiving this cell, the IPO forwards the encrypted inner payload to the onion service via an \texttt{INTRODUCE2} cell. The hidden service then decrypts this instruction block using its blinded private key, validates its integrity, draws out the RPO's routoing parameters alongside the 20-byte rendezvous cookie
and establishes a separate three-hop circuit to the specified RPO relay by submitting a \texttt{RENDEZVOUS1} cell embedded with the matching rendezvous cookie. The RPO finally splices the incoming circuit into a unified six-hoop transparent pipeline facilitating end-to-end forward-secure data streams protected with 
symmetric encryption keys safeguarding against node sniffing or network-wide traffic correlation attack \cite{tor_rendspec}.

\section{Related Work}

Prior research on the Tor version 3 (V3) hidden service directory divides into measurement studies characterise how directory relays and onion descriptors behave in the live network, whereas attack studies exploit the directory to disrupt or deanonymise the services that relies on it. This section groups the closest prior 
works by their relationship to the measurement framework. It separates the studies that provide direct methodological basis from those that explain why particular measurements were designed the way they are.

The closest methodological predecessor is the \texttt{sybilhunter} system of Winter et al.~\cite{winter2016sybil}, which mined roughly nine years of archived consensuses and router descriptors to expose coordinated relay groups that are operated by a single entity. Three of its techniques map onto analyses reproduced 
here. Its churn technique quantifies the joining and leaving of relays between consecutive consensuses using an adapted Godfrey formula split into separate join and leave series~\cite{winter2016sybil}, the same concept computed underlying the per-flag churn distributions. Its uptime technique renders relay availability 
as binary online/offline sequences and sorts them with single-linkage clustering under a Pearson-correlation distance so that synchronised reboot relays become visually adjacent~\cite{winter2016sybil}, which is the clustered uptime matrix that the present work recomputes for the tracked HSDir population. 
Its fingerprint technique tracks relays that repeatedly change their identity fingerprint from a fixed address, an indicative of attempts to occupy chosen positions in the descriptor hash ring~\cite{winter2016sybil}, and this directly parallels the fingerprint-change tracking applied here to the same end. 
That study also records the flag thresholds, the \texttt{HSDir} flag requires more than ninety-six hours of uptime and that a large share of running relays act as directories~\cite{winter2016sybil}. Its evidence precedes the period in which V3 became common, so its data is not V3-specific, but the analytical methodology 
transfers unchanged to the V3 directory studied here.
 
The most empirical basis comes from H\"oller et al.~\cite{hoeller2021statev3}, who operated a family of fifty relays admitted to the hidden service directory in order to observe V3 blinded-key uploads and downloads from a controlled postion. The work extrapolates the number of active V3 services from 
the fraction of the directory it observes, and it reports a prominent asymmetry between publication and retrieval: a large majority of uploaded blinded public keys were never fetched from the authors' relays, and a majority of received download requests could not be answered because the corresponding descriptor 
had never been uploaded~\cite{hoeller2021statev3}. The upload-versus-fetch persistence histograms in that study are the model on which the descriptor upload and fetch persistence analysis of this thesis is based. Equally relevant is its privacy-preserving measurement design, in which timestamps are truncated and 
stored blinded keys are reordered before analysis to prevent the data from enabling correlation against individual users~\cite{hoeller2021statev3}. The same constraints guide the non-intrusive collection strategy here.
 
A second group of prior art does not provide methodology but motivates the specific stability measurements this thesis performs. H\"oller and Mayrhofer~\cite{hoeller2024ddos} document a distributed denial-of-service (DoS) incident against two relays belonging to the same fifty-relay directory project, in which memory 
exhaustion forced the targeted relays to reboot. Because the \texttt{HSDir} flag depends on sustained uptime, both relays lost that flag as a direct consequence of the reboot, and the disruption was not visible through the metrics that the relays report about themselves~\cite{hoeller2024ddos}. This is precisely the 
context that the stripped-flag churn analysis of this thesis is designed to characterise, and the broader account of relays disappearing and returning mid-experiment, with uptime reset in the process, also supports the general churn and uptime-matrix analyses. From an unrelated research group, Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2024hsdirsniper} 
describe \texttt{HSDirSniper}, an attack that floods a responsible directory's descriptor cache within a single hour so that its aging mechanism purges every stored descriptor, portraiting an arbitrary hidden service unreachable while the service itself observes nothing unusual~\cite{zhang2024hsdirsniper}. That study matters 
as a complementary threat model. It establishes a concrete mechanism by which a directory relay listed as healthy may hold no valid descriptor for a service, which is one reason the silent-failure and upload-versus-fetch observations gathered by this thesis are 
treated as security-relevant signals and not merely descriptive statistics.

A more distant art, but relevant to the design of the probe population, is the provisioning-time study of H\"oller et al.~\cite{hoeller2021shortlived}. It measures the latency of deploying an onion service and finds that the transition to V3 reduced deployment to a few seconds, with an overwhelming share of 
that time spent uploading the descriptor to the directory~\cite{hoeller2021shortlived}. Among the configurations it measures is the ephemeral onion service, created through the control protocol and existing only while the controlling connection remains open~\cite{hoeller2021shortlived}. This study motivates why 
cycled ephemeral onions are worth including in the study population of this thesis. It establishes that short-lived, dynamically generated services are a realistic use of the V3 directory, so probing the hash ring with daily-rotated ephemeral endpoints samples a genuine class of service rather than an artificial 
one. Nonetheless, it is not the source of any measurement method employed here.

Finally, the operator-diversity analysis of Sendner et al.~\cite{sendner2025sybil} forms a distinct research thread. Working from consensus and descriptor data, that study groups relays by declared family and related attributes, and shows that a small number of operators concentrate a large share of network capacity,
to the point that strategically deployed sybil families can occupy both circuit endpoints for a quantifiable share of traffic~\cite{sendner2025sybil}. Its family-based grouping corresponds to the declared-family and family-identifier clustering used in this thesis to detect coordinated directory operators. It also 
provides an important caution: evaluating \texttt{sybilhunter}~\cite{winter2016sybil}, the same detector whose techniques this thesis adapts, that study reports that a prepared adversary can evade detection by diversifying nicknames, contact information, addresses, uptimes, and ports~\cite{sendner2025sybil}. Family-based 
and configuration-based sybil detection is therefore treated here as an indicator of coordination rather than as a complete defence.
 
In summary, this prior art measures the V3 directory from a fixed relay postion, attacks it, or analyses relay-family concentration across the network as a whole. The present work instead tracks the directory from the perspective of monitored services and relays simultaneously, correlating node-level uptime and 
stability, churn and flag churn, hash-ring position, and family-based sybil indicators over a continuous measurement window. % TODO: this novelty/gap sentence is an absence-of-work claim -- verify against the wider V3 literature before asserting it; none of the six cited works individually supports "no prior study combines these", so it is stated as this thesis's positioning, not attributed to a source.

\section{Methodology}

To investigate and analyze the architectural security, runtime, stability, behaviour and Sybil deployments within Tor version 3 (V3) HSDirs, a non-intrusive and reliable tracking framework is designed. Rather than relying in on historical third-party directory archives which lack granularity, an automated and concurrent 
program was developed and executed during one-month time-period. The measurement infrastructure interacts directly with a local Tor instance via its asynchronous control port pipeline \cite{tor_controlspec}. The core approach of this methodology is the real-time interception of routing descriptors and propagation, track individual relay flag 
churn, state fluctuations, availability on the HRT and isolate structural network identifiers from the live network.

\subsection{Concurrent System Architecture and State Synchronization}

The measurement framework operates continuously over a set of decoupled multi-threaded structure to isolate telemetry vectors and maintain a minimal overload and performance of Tor's control port socket. The high throughput and directory update rates across the network would bring forth thread blockages and risk frame 
drops on the asynchronous controller stream in a single-threaded architecture. Consequently, the architecture separates its duties across five autonomous and persistent threads under a strict lock-synchronized schema to prevent race conditions and transaction deadlocks during continuous disk write operations.

To prevent filesystem I/O bottlenecks when logging dense network events, a collection of transaction-safe locks are instantiated and uniquely mapped to individual JSON lines (\texttt{.jsonl}) logging files. At the same time, independent memory-state locks are allocated to manage runtime mutations to shared memory containers,
preventing data corruption when multiple monitoring components concurrently access or modify relay data.

The framework restricts its data collection to all available relays on the network, a distinct target collection containing controlled static endpoints, a selected third-party endpoint probes, and a scalable pool of ephemeral addresses generated dynamically at runtime. By monitoring this mixed collection of hidden service 
categories, the system ensures comprehensive coverage across distinct regions of the HRT.

A five-thread colletion pipeline splits with each thread targeting a specific operational routine:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Statful Consensus Auditing and Sybil Vector Extraction:} These threads are driven by two independent consensus tracking routines, network-wide consensus monitor and tracked HSDirs status monitor. Triggered hourly at XX:05 UTC, these modules ingest the global and tracked directory network consensus information to map the state of active global and tracked relays. The audit engine reads the microdescriptor headers of every operational relay flagged with \texttt{HSDir, Stable} and \texttt{Fast} properties \cite{tor_dirspec}, mapping their IP addresses, nickname, open Onion Routing (OR) ports, and cryptographic fingerprints. Implemented with a microdescriptor parser bypass, sybil identity markers and flag clustered relays operated by coordinated entities are extracted with the capture of raw declared family lists and cryptographic \texttt{famil\_ids} vectors. Tracked active node that disappear from the consensus directory accumulates hourly absenses considering an offline churn; a consecutive absense count of $\geq$ 2 sets off an state reassignment to a dead-offline churn, capturing long-lived relay drop rates.
    \item \textbf{Active Outbound Prober:} A dedicated prober operating on a fixed periodic 20-minute loop. The mechanism randomizes the target request queue and dispatches raw asynchronous \texttt{HSFETCH} instruction lookups directly to the control port socket \cite{tor_controlspec}. These queries bypass Tor's internal circuit-level memory dependencies within the local Tor client, forcing the daemon to query teh remote directory nodes directly. This method discloses silent failues where a node listed as online cannot serve requested descriptors. To achieve complete coverage a two-tier lookup is triggered, a standard dynamic hash ring resolution, and a direct poll targeted at individual fingerprints historically recorded in memory as active hosting relays. These lookup patterns compels the local Tor daemon to query the remote relays directly, causing an asynchronous event listener to log the resulting network events.
	\item \textbf{Asynchronous Event Listener:} Thread instantiated to establish a persistent, isolated, direct subscription to the controller's internal \texttt{HS\_DESC} stream event pipeline, which tracks real-time descriptor uploads, fetch requests, and operational failures \cite{tor_controlspec}.
    \item \textbf{Scaled Ephemeral Manager:} To scale the observation scope and neutralize spatial placement bias inherent to a set of static domains, this runtime allocation script automatically invokes Tor control commands to dynamically deploy fifty detached , ephemeral hidden service endpoints and cycled at regular daily intervals \cite{tor_controlspec}. These generated cryptographic addresses are automatically appended to the active tracking set memory, and spreaded across the HRT ensuring broader statistical sampling across the decentralized DHT ring space.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Hash Ring Mapping and Low-Level Protocol Parsing}

A significant engineering barrier rised upon the degradation of the primary Python control library \texttt{Stem} for several years since June 2023 \cite{stem_github}, which lacks support of native parsing tailored to the modern version 3 onion service architecture. To bypass this limitation without sacrificing data granularity, 
custom protocol-level bypasses were introduced. Instead of using the broken library abstractions, the script builds isolated channels to prevent control port command congestion and interact directly with the raw socket stream. 

While the targeted consensus auditing can directly query (\texttt{.get\_microdescriptor()}) and partially parse relay consensus streams, the global consensus audit downloads unparsed consensus streams via raw \texttt{get\_info("dir/status-vote/current/consensus-micr} \texttt{odesc")} \cite{tor_controlspec}. It then extracts critical
metrics and processes the text files using custom-built parsing that manually extracts the global SRV, decodes base64-encoded relay components, and handles raw-byte structures to compute the 32-byte HRT coordinate positions \cite{tor_dirspec}.

These bypasses allows the framework to replicate the 32-byte DHT ring topology, which by capturing the global SRV and individual relay identity keys ($\textit{K}_{\text{ID}}$) from the consensus
, and parsing the current time period ($\textit{T}_{\text{p}}$), calculates the precise position index of every directory node using native SHA3-256 hashing routine \cite{tor_rendspec}:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}_{\text{index}} = \text{SHA3-256}\big(\textit{K}_{\text{ID}} \parallel \textit{T}_{\text{p}} \parallel \textit{SRV} \big)
\end{equation}

The tracked consensus contrary to the network-wide consensus exclusively extracts the unique 40-character hexadecimal hash ring coordinate (\texttt{HSDIR\_INDEX}), the base64-encoded blinded public descriptor identifier which requires knowing the target 35-byte onion service address, and the server-side validation 
error reasons from the asynchronous event listener, which populates the shared memory container for targeted directory nodes. Then evaluates the clockwise distance $\Delta$ between a directory's coordinate $\textit{I}_{\text{HSDir}}$ on the HRT and the target onion service descriptor index $\textit{I}_{\text{onion}}$ 
across the circular ring space $\mathcal{M} = 2^{256}$:

\begin{equation}
\Delta = \left( I_{\text{HSDir}} - I_{\text{onion}} \right) \pmod{2^{256}}
\end{equation}

This distance is subsequently converted into a normalized position percentage relative to the total ring space to chart wether monitored directories are distributed uniformly or clustered around specific identities.

\subsection{Temporal Synchronization and Boundary Isolation}

A foundational requisite of the measurement system is the management of daily SRV transaction and the subsequent reshuffling of the hash ring coordinates and active blinded keys, which Tor V3 protocol enforces the rotation exactly at 00:00 UTC \cite{tor_srvspec, tor_rendspec}.

To prevent data contamination from overlapping consensus epochs, the framework
enforces a strict exclusion boundary window spanning from 23:55 UTC to 00:07 UTC. During this interval, live network probes and live event captures are suspended or systematically filtered.

To provide directory authorities sufficient time to settle their consensus voting and distribute the updated SRV across the network, the framework uses staggered offsets to resume tracking:

Old ephemeral service nodes are terminated and newly deployed at 00:01 UTC. Precisely at 00:05 UTC, upon verifying a calendar day or month rollover, the active runtime directory nodes on 
shared memory, network-wide and targeted, are state verified on the new calendar rollover over a script routine, archived to a disk \texttt{.jsonl} log file, flushed from shared memory, and
re-initialized to a blank vector slate. Active outbound network probes resume at 00:08 UTC, and tracked and network-wide consensus audits re-engaged at 00:10 UTC. 

This synchronized time structure ensures that all local tracking states, rotated blinded keys, and newly designated remote directory authorities have fully stabilized across the network 
before data collection resumes, preserving high-fidelity of long-term churn measurements.

\subsection{Structural Sybil Footprint Identification}\label{subsec:sybil-footprint}

An analytical objective is differentiate random, isolated relay failures from systematic disruptions managed by large-scale coordinated node operators. When a monitored HSDir drops from the network
or fails to serve valid descriptor, the framework collects structural identity markers to detect potential Sybil concentrations based on selected criteria:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Network Prefix Aggregation:} The framework maps IPv4 addresses of every directory node  and into standard \texttt{/24} subnets. This grouping isolates cases where a single operator deploys multiple discrete directory nodes into the consensus within identical data center allocations or IP routing subnets to artificially influence a section of the HRT.
    \item \textbf{Declared and Effective Administrative Families:} The microdescriptor records are parsed hourly to extract both declared relay family identifiers and mutual recognized effective family lists \cite{tor_dirspec}. This tracks evaluate compliance with Tor's administrative policies and flags undeclared Sybil networks that exhibit identical uptime profiles or infrastructure features while omitting formal administrative declarations.
\end{itemize}

\section{Evaluation}
 
This section presents the observational results of the one-month measurement campaign and is organised as a single sequence from the measurement basis to increasingly specific findings. It commences
by establishing what was observed and how the observations are scoped and filtered, so that every later result can be read against a common baseline. It then characterises the reliability of 
individual directory relays through their uptime and stability, followed by their churn and flag dynamics, which together describe how single nodes behave over time. Only afterwards does the analysis 
group relays into operators and quantify sybil concentration, since deciding whether a failure affects one node or a whole operator requires the per-node picture to be in place first. The final 
subsections survey the position of directories on the hash ring and, at best granularity, to the upload and fetch behaviour of individual descriptors. The ordering is deliberate, each 
subsection introduces the concepts that the next relies on, and the unit of analysis widens step by step from the relay snapshot, to the operator, to the ring geometry, and finally to the descriptor.
 
\subsection{Measurement Basis and Scope}
 
The analysis draws on five recorded data streams, each capturing a distinct view of the directory over the observation window. The \textit{network-wide} consensus stream records hourly snapshots of 
every directory relay listed in the consensus, providing the population-level baseline. The \textit{tracked} consensus stream records the same hourly snapshots but restricted to the directories 
responsible for the study's own set of probed onion services, so that directory behaviour can be observed from the perspective of specific services. Two further streams, the \textit{network-wide rotation-check} stream 
and the \textit{tracked rotation-check} stream, hold the daily-rotated snapshots used to assure stability against the deliberately cycled node set, mirroring the two populations above. 
Finally, the \textit{ring-event} probe log records descriptor-level upload and fetch events observed for the tracked onions, and is the only stream that operates at the granularity of individual 
blinded public keys rather than relays.
 
Two reporting conventions apply throughout the section. First, almost every analysis is computed for two \textit{contexts}: the network-wide population drawn from the network-wide consensus stream, and 
the tracked population drawn from the tracked consensus stream. Second, within the tracked context the results are also broken down by \textit{service type}, of which there are three: static controlled 
services (\texttt{STATIC\_CONTROL}) as SC constituting 50 services, ephemeral services (\texttt{EPHEMERAL\_VARIABLE}) as EV constituting 50 daily-rotated services, and third-party probed 
services (\texttt{THIRD\_PARTY\_PROBE}) as TP constituting 13 services. Unless said otherwise, results are reported for both contexts, with the tracked context further split by service type.
 
The observation window spans one month of continuous hourly collection. To keep the temporal axis independent of the calendar dates, each distinct calendar date is normalised to a 
sequential label of the form ``Day~$N$'', ordered chronologically, and all per-day results are called on these labels.
 
A single filtering convention dictates which observations count as a genuine presence of a relay in a given snapshot, as opposed to a transient gap in collection. Each observation carries a count 
of consecutive hourly absences, and the standard filter retains observations with at most one such absence, denoting a single missed snapshot as a true disappearance measurement rather than the 
consecutive serving as confirmation. A stricter variant of the filter admits only observations with zero consecutive absences, used by the analyses whose correctness depends on an uninterrupted presence signal, 
namely the churn analysis, the per-relay identity-change analyses, and the undeclared-family graph analysis.

One measurement limitation is established before any per-service-type result is reported, because it governs how those results must be read. In the tracked data a single row represents the group of a relays 
within the probed onion services, not one row per physical relay, so a relay that acts as directory for onions of more than one service type is counted once within each service-type subset it graps while 
contributing only once to the overall total. The per-service-type unique-relay counts therefore overlap and are not additive. A dedicated diagnostic quantifies this overlap directly, by collecting for 
each relay the set of distinct service types it was ever observed under and counting the exact combinations. The diagnostic is meaningful only where a service-type label exists, therefore it is to not run 
on the network-wide population, which carries no such label, but it is run on both the tracked consensus stream and the tracked rotation-check stream, since the per-service-type breakdowns of both 
populations require the same limitation to be read correctly.

\subsection{HSDir Reliability: Uptime and Stability}
 
This subsection reports the reliability of the observed directories over the observation window, understood as how
long relays remain active and how stable the deliberately cycled population is from one day to the next. Proceeds from the most direct
visual of presence over time, through the duration statistics that quantify it, to the rotation-check viewpoint on the cycled population.
 
The uptime matrix is the most direct rendering of which relay is present and when. Each column is a distinct relay and each row a consecutive snapshot, ordered
with the earliest at the top. Every cell is coloured by one of three states, black marks an active relay in the ring at that snapshot, white marks a 
relay that went offline listed in the consensus, and purple marks an absent relay from the consensus.
 
Two representations of this same matrix are given for every context and service type, differing only in how the columns are ordered. In the plain representation
the columns follow first-appearance order with each relay placed left to right by when it first entered the population, this ordering carries no 
behavioural meaning, and the resulting diagonal purple boundary is merely an artifact of sorting the relays by arrival time. In the clustered represenation 
columns are instead reordered so that relays with similar uptime sequences sit adjacent. The correlation between every pair of relays' uptime 
sequences over the whole window is computed, converted to a distance of one minus the Pearson correlation, and passed through single-linkage 
hierarchical clustering, with the columns laid out in the resulting order. Two relays that rise and fall in unison are thereby placed side by 
side, whereas two behaving independently are separated. This reordering is what enables the one labeling the plain matrix cannot support, any run of 
five or more adjacent columns whose uptime sequences are exactly identical is recoloured red. Such sequences are only detectable once clustering has 
gathered identically behaving relays together; in first-appearance order they would be dispersed and invisible. The plain matrix therefore answers ``who 
was up, when,'' while the clustered matrix regroups the same cells to answer ``which relays move together.'' The network-wide pair is shown in

\subsection{HSDir Churn and Flag Dynamics}
 
Where the previous subsection measured how long directories remain present, this one measures how they transition between states. The churn 
engine classifies every entity at each hourly snapshot as one of four mutually informative events, \emph{new}, \emph{disappeared}, 
\emph{reconnected} or \emph{stripped} of its HSDir flag, with transitions never resetting at day boundaries, and reports each as both a count 
and a rate per snapshot. Two further analyses build on upon it, the ring-event action rate distribution, which adds a chronological view of directory 
activity on the tracked population, and the stripped flag analysis, which examines what else is happening to a relay's flags at the 
moment its HSDir flag is lost. Following the convention of the previous subsection, the rate variant is the primary quantity of interest, the 
corresponding count variant exist as supplementary material but are not reproduced here.

The most apparent feature of every rate plot is a strong periodicity. The \emph{new}, \emph{disappeared} and \emph{reconnected} lines spike 
sharply and regularly, and those spikes line up with day boundaries throughout the month rather than falling at random. This is structural 
rather than anomalous, and it has a concrete protocol explanation. As discussed in subsection ~\ref{subsec:pub-n-index}, the directory authority 
consensus derives a fresh shared random value once every 1440-minute epoch, compiled at 00:00~UTC, and this value drives a pseudo-random 
reshuffle of the entire hash ring~\cite{tor_srvspec}. Since each relay’s ring position is recalculated relative to a value that changes daily, 
the refresh can cause many relays to appear to enter, leave, or re-enter a given region of the directory space, hence a substantial proportion of 
daily transitions naturally occurs at the daily refresh boundary. The spikes are thus best read as the visible signature of the daily ring 
reshuffle, not as a succession of independent disruptions. The network-wide rate plot is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:churn_rate_network}, the

\subsection{Operator Identity and Sybil Concentration}
 
The prior two subsections characterised directories individually. This one groups them into operators, which is what the main objective requires 
in order to decide, when a directory fails, whether a single node or a whole operator has gone down. Operators are approximated here by four 
structural indicators, the declared family identifier, IP identity (both exact address and \texttt{/24} subnet), per-nickname identity mutation, and 
the mutual-recognition graph of undeclared families. All counts use the stricter presence filter established earlier, admitting only observations 
with zero consecutive absences. The full top-five rankings for every indicator and population are tabulated in the appendix. The prose below reports 
only the findings that hold across contexts.
 
One declared family dominates every population. The same family identifier, beginning \texttt{ri96phwtVtkwug/}\ldots{} and hereafter the 
leading family, is the top group by unique-node count in every single context and service type: it holds 775 nodes network-wide (9.82\% share), and 
its share does not diminish as the population narrows but climbs, reaching 10.44\% in the tracked context and 13.54\% within TP specifically. No 
other family approaches it, and the second-ranked group in any population sits near or below 4\%. This concentration, one family accounting for roughly 
a tenth of the entire directory population and more of the probed subset, headline operator-identity result of this subsection, and it is 
visible directly in Figure~\ref{fig:family_id_sybil}, where the leading family's per-day count and share track well above every other group across 
the whole window.
 
\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/network_family_id_sybil.png}}
\caption{Per-day unique-node count and network share for the top declared-family-identifier groups, network-wide.}
\label{fig:family_id_sybil}
\end{figure}
 
The leading family dominates partial drops but never a total drop. In the operator-level drop analysis, which classifies each family's 
per-snapshot behaviour as a \emph{partial} drop (some member nodes fell) or a \emph{total} drop (the entire family went dark at once), the leading 
family tops the partial-drop ranking in every population, yet it appears in no total-drop top-five. This is a statistical consequence of its 
size rather than a sign of instability. A family of more than 700 nodes will almost always have \emph{some} member offline at any given snapshot, 
so partial drops are near-constant for it, whereas a total drop would require all 700-plus nodes to vanish simultaneously, a far rarer event whose 
rankings are instead dominated by small families for which bringing down every member requires only a relatively minor failure. Partial and total 
drop behaviour therefore measure different things for a group this large, and the leading family's absence from the total-drop rankings does not imply 
greater stability than smaller groups.
 
IP concentration is negligible at the exact address but substantial at the \texttt{/24} subnet, with the same subnets recur throughout.
Grouping directories by exact IP address reveals almost no concentration, with the busiest single address holding only 8--16 nodes in any population, never 
more than 0.3\% share. Grouping the same directories by \texttt{/24} subnet shows a contrast, with the top subnets reaching 150--320 nodes 
and 2--4.5\% share, and the same handful of subnets, \texttt{204.8.96.0/24}, \texttt{64.65.62.0/24}, \texttt{96.9.98.0/24}, \texttt{64.65.1.0/24} 
and \texttt{192.42.116.0/24}, recurring across the network-wide context, the tracked context and all three service types. Figures~\ref{fig:ip_sybil_exact} and~\ref{fig:ip_sybil_subnet} 
place the two groupings side by side for the network-wide context, the address plot is limited to single-digit node counts, while the subnet 
plot for the same population rises two orders of magnitude higher. This pattern, strong subnet-level concentration with no individual address concentration, 
is exactly what an operator spreading nodes across many distinct addresses within a single allocation, and it fills the gap left by the 
\texttt{/24}-based structural grouping described in the Methodology section~\ref{subsec:sybil-footprint}, therefore grouping isolates operators who reuse a 
subnet, but nothing forces an operator to reuse an exact address, and prior work notes that a prepared adversary can deliberately diversify addresses 
to evade configuration-based detection~\cite{sendner2025sybil}.

\subsection{Hash-Ring Position}
 
The structural question asked is where directories sit on the hash ring and how much of it they occupy, two analyses cover it. A 
ring-position uniformity analysis, which measures whether observed ring indices are spread evenly or clustered, and a coverage analysis, which measures 
what fraction of the ring is covered at all. Both are computed for the two absolute ring-index metrics, the HSDir index (the ring position of the 
responsible relays) and the onion index (the ring position of the onions' own descriptor identifiers). The relative onion-to-HSDir distance 
metric is included in the uniformity analysis but not the coverage one, for which a relative distance has no meaning.
 
Ring-position placement is uniform everywhere, as the protocol predicts. A relay's ring index is a SHA3-256 hash of its identity, the shared random 
value and the epoch, and an onion's index is derived the same way from its blinded key~\cite{tor_rendspec, tor_dirspec}. A well-distributed hash spreads 
its outputs evenly across the ring, so uniform placement is the expected result and confirming it empirically is the point. For every population examined, 
the measured cumulative distribution function of the normalized ring index closely tracks the ideal uniform diagonal. Figure~\ref{fig:ecdf_network} shows the 
network-wide HSDir-index case, whose empirical curve is visually indistinguishable from the ideal line. The one curve that departs visibly from a smooth 
diagonal is TP (Figure~\ref{fig:ecdf_tp}), which is noticeably steppier, but it still follows the diagonal, and the steppedness is 
a sample-size artifact of drawing a curve from far fewer points rather than any genuine departure from uniformity. Uniformity therefore holds even for the 
smallest population.

\caption{Empirical distribution function of the normalized HSDir-index ring position, network-wide. The empirical curve sits on the ideal uniform diagonal, confirming even placement across the ring.}

Daily churn and ring-position dispersion are unrelated. The uniformity analysis also pairs each day's total HSDir churn against how dispersed that day's 
ring positions were. Across every population the fitted trend is flat or near-flat, with the network-wide slope at roughly $0.003$ (Figure~\ref{fig:dispersion_network}). 
How many directories joined or left on a given day carries essentially no relationship to how spread out that day's positions were. This is a null result 
with no further elaboration.

Ring coverage revisits a pattern seen twice already. The month-wide aggregate figure and the daily-average figure diverge sharply. 
Coverage is measured at a fixed resolution, discretizing the normalized ring into ten thousand equal-width buckets and reporting the fraction of buckets 
touched, both across the whole month and averaged over per-day values. For the HSDir index, the month-wide coverage saturates near 
completeness for the network, tracked, EV and SC populations, between 93.42\% and 100.00\%, while the corresponding daily-average coverage sits far lower, 
around 30--37\% for the network and tracked contexts and 9--18\% for EV and SC. As in the uptime and churn subsections, this divergence is expected rather 
than contradictory since only a fraction of the month's distinct directories are active on any single day, therefore daily coverage is limited, yet the full month's 
population is large enough to eventually touch nearly every bucket. The exact percentages are given in Table~\ref{tab:coverage}.
 
TP is the exception, and it is the sharpest illustration so far of the small-population limitation that has recurred throughout this section. 
Alone among the populations, its month-wide HSDir-index coverage does not even reach 30\% (28.98\%), whereas every other population saturates near 100\%. 
The responsible HSDir population that the probed onions touch is small enough that, even cumulatively across the entire month, it cannot approach full ring 
coverage. The remaining uncovered fraction is not merely a per-day effect but a whole-month one.
 
Onion-index coverage is far lower than HSDir-index coverage throughout, with the tracked context reaching 42.19\% against the HSDir index's 
99.97\%. This is an expected scale effect rather than a concern, there are always far fewer distinct onion identities than distinct responsible directories, 
so the upper bound on how much of the ring the onion index can touch is lower even when pooled across the whole month. Within that lower upper bound, TP's onion-index 
coverage (4.20\%) again sits well below EV's and SC's (18.66\% and 18.98\%), consistent with TP's much smaller probed-onion population noted earlier.

\subsection{Descriptor Hosting: Upload/Fetch Persistence}
 
The final analysis moves in granularity, from the relay to the descriptor. It operates on the ring-event probe log at the level of the individual 
blinded public key, grouped by the composite descriptor key that pairs each blinded key with its onion address, and it exists only for the tracked population, 
since descriptor-level upload and fetch events are recorded only for the onions this study operates or probes. Three views, the per-descriptor upload 
and fetch counts, their distinct-hourly-bucket complement, and a silent-failure classification that flags descriptors published but never retrieved, or retrieved 
but never published. The per-descriptor totals, hourly-persistence figures and silent-failure percentages for the tracked population and each service type are 
collected in the appendix.
 
The distribution of upload counts per descriptor is strongly bimodal, consistent with prior reported measurements. Most descriptors are uploaded only a handful of times, 
resulting in a pronounced peak at the lower end of the histogram, but a second substantial cluster occurs in the uppermost truncated interval, with roughly one-third of the tracked 
descriptors exhibit high persistence rather than a gradual decline in upload frequency. Figure~\ref{fig:uf_hist_tracked} shows this for the tracked population. 
This bimodal pattern echoes the related V3 measurement study, whose blinded-public-key upload and fetch distributions similarly distinguishes descriptors uploaded only a few times 
from a separate cluster that is persistently republished around the observed mean~\cite{hoeller2021statev3}. Although the measurement setups differ and the numerical values are therefore 
not directly comparable, both studies exhibit a similar distributional shape.

A secondary feature of these histograms distinguishes the two owned service types. In the SC population, fetches as well as uploads cluster in the same upper truncated

with fetches remaining concentrated near zero. A cautious interpretation is that static, persistent onion services accumulate repeat client fetches over time, whereas 
daily-rotating ephemeral services are too short-lived to develop comparable traffic. The corresponding service-type histograms are provided in the appendix.
 
The classification of silent failures follows directly from the owned–probed distinction, which is intrinsic to its definition. Figure~\ref{fig:silent_failure} shows 
the two silent-failure categories by service type. The two owned service types, EV and SC, occupy almost the entirety of the ``uploaded, never fetched'' category 
and are essentially absent from ``fetched, never uploaded'', whereas TP is the mirror image, occupying ``fetched, never uploaded'' and egistering no instances in ``uploaded, never fetched.'' 
This pattern follows structurally from the probing model. TP services are only fetched, never uploaded, because the infrastructure does not operate them. Consequently, 
TP records no failed or maximum uploads and no descriptors in the ``uploaded, never fetched`` category, while 95.88\% fall into ``fetched, never uploaded``, a definitional

\section{Conclusions}
 
This study observed a mixed population of static, ephemeral and third-party onion services over a continuous one-month window, together with the network-wide 
directory consensus, in order to characterise the reliability, operator structure and hosting behaviour of modern V3 hidden service directories and, on that 
basis, to address the central question of whether a directory failure reflects a single node going down or a whole operator.

A single structural cause explains the third-party population's behaviour throughout. The TP's services behaved distinctly in every 
analysis, with the shortest uptime sessions (Table~\ref{tab:uptime_durations}), the highest proportional churn rates (Tables~\ref{tab:churn_hourly} and~\ref{tab:churn_daily}), 
the clearest example of the coverage-population size effect, with an incompleteness ring coverage (Table~\ref{tab:coverage}), 
and no upload activity whatsoever in either ring events or descriptor-hosting. These is but an independent result, 
since the third-party set covers a far smaller probed population, thirteen onions against fifty each for the two owned 
types, so every join-departure is a proportionally larger movement and every distribution is drawn from fewer points. And by definition, the infrastructure never 
controls the services, so its descriptor-hosting profile is one of fetches without uploads by construction. Read together, the third-party results form a small, 
structurally distinct control group whose differences indicate that the measurement behaves as designed rather than revealing anomalies in the directories themselves

Aggregate stability coexists with daily volatility across the measured population. A recurring pattern was observed independently in the uptime, churn and ring-coverage 
results, whereas the monthly measures indicated near-complete stability, the corresponding daily measures reflected substantially lower stability. Month-wide ring coverage 
nears completeness for the network and owned populations while daily coverage remains far lower (Table~\ref{tab:coverage}). The rotated-directory population is highly 
stable in aggregate, at 97.25\% active for the tracked set against 96.16\% network-wide (Table~\ref{tab:rotated_stability}), even as churn events cluster closely at 
the daily consensus boundary. This divergence is not a contradiction but a property of the population itself. Individual directories change daily, yet the population is 
large and persistent enough to remain collectively stable and to eventually covers nearly the entire hash ring. A single-day snapshot therefore underestimates the 
directory service’s long-term stability, an important distinction when assessing system availability.

The primarily question has a direct answer, operator-scale presence is real and detectable, but does not translate into ring-position control. On one hand, 
operator-scale sybil presence is evident and measurable through structural clustering, one declared family dominates every population holding roughly a tenth 
of the entire directory set and a larger share as the population narrows (Tables~\ref{tab:famid_count_network} and~\ref{tab:famid_share_network}). And a small, 
recurring set of \texttt{/24} subnets concentrates directories across every context while exact-address concentration stays negligible, consistent with an operator 
distributing nodes across multiple addresses within the same allocation. On the other hand, this concentration does not translate into disproportionate control of 
the ring. Position placement remains uniform even for populations with strong sybil concentration (Figure~\ref{fig:ecdf_network}), as the SHA3-256 derivation of ring 
indices predicts~\cite{tor_rendspec, tor_dirspec}. The operator-level drop analysis clarifies the source of failures. The dominant family tops the \emph{partial}-drop 
rankings in every population yet appears in no \emph{total}-drop ranking, whereas total drops are dominated by small families. A failure confined to a few directories 
of the dominant family is therefore the common case and reflects single-node churn within a large operator, while a whole-operator outage of that family was never 
observed. Total drops, when they occur, belong to small operators for whom losing every node is a minor event. Family and subnet clustering make it possible to distinguish 
single-node failures from operator-level failures, with the largest operator’s Sybil presence appearing primarily as distributed partial churn rather than as concentrated 
control over ring positions or coordinated, population-wide failure.

All together, these findings indicate that the modern V3 directory population is, at network scale, reliable, uniformly distributed and resistant to any single 
operator dominating the ring, nonetheless the population exhibits a distinct large-operator footprint whose failures appear as persistent partial churn. Combining 
consensus auditing with active descriptor probing, proved operator structure and silent hosting failures that would remain undetected through consensus analysis alone, 
representing the study’s principal contribution. The study is limited by its scope, a single one-month window, a probed set of tens rather than hundreds of onions, and an 
operator-attribution approach resting on declared families and network-prefix clustering, which a deliberately evasive operator could work to obscure. Future work should therefore
extend the observation window, expand the probed population, and validate structural operator indicators against behavioural evidence.
